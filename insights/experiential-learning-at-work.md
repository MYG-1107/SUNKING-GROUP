## Why experience alone is not enough

People gain experience every day, but experience does not automatically become learning. A project that went well may contain useful lessons that remain unspoken. A difficult customer interaction may be repeated because the team never examines what caused the problem. A workshop may be forgotten when participants return to a workplace that gives them no opportunity to practise.

Experiential learning makes the process more deliberate. Learners engage with an activity, reflect on what happened, connect their observations with useful concepts and test a new approach.

## Four connected stages

The experiential learning cycle commonly associated with David Kolb is described through four stages:

1. **Concrete experience:** take part in a task, simulation, case exercise or real workplace activity.
2. **Reflective observation:** consider what happened, what was expected and what was different.
3. **Abstract conceptualisation:** connect the observations with a framework, principle or explanation.
4. **Active experimentation:** apply the learning to a new situation and observe the result.

The stages are connected rather than a rigid sequence. A learner may return to an earlier stage as new information emerges.

## A practical workplace example

Imagine a project team that misses a delivery milestone. Instead of moving immediately to blame, the team can map the sequence of events, identify where information or decisions were delayed, compare the experience with a project-management framework and agree one change to test in the next cycle.

The value is not the meeting itself. It comes from a focused question, an honest review, a useful concept and an action that can be observed.

## How to build a useful activity

Start with a learning outcome that can be described in plain language. Choose an activity that gives participants a safe way to practise. Reserve time for reflection rather than rushing to the next topic. Introduce a framework that helps learners interpret what happened. End with a specific action that participants can try in their own context.

Managers can support transfer by discussing the action after the session, giving participants an opportunity to practise and recognising progress.

## How to evaluate learning

Attendance and satisfaction surveys are not enough to establish changed workplace performance. A proportionate evaluation can combine a pre-session confidence check, observation of a practical activity, participant reflection and a follow-up review of the agreed action. Where an organisation wants to measure operational impact, it should define a reliable baseline and consider other factors that may affect the result.

## Further reading

A foundational reference is David A. Kolb, *Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development* (1984). SUNKING GROUP uses the cycle here as a learning-design framework; this article does not claim that one training method is best for every learner or situation.
