## 1. Start with a focused research question

A useful research question identifies the issue to be examined and helps determine what evidence is needed. Review existing literature before finalising the question. Explain why the topic matters, what is already known and what the proposed work may add.

Do not frame a project around a preferred conclusion. A sound design should allow findings to challenge the researcher's assumptions.

## 2. Match the method to the question

Choose qualitative, quantitative or mixed methods based on the question, research context and available evidence. Explain the sampling approach, instruments, procedures and analysis plan. A method should be suitable for the claim being made; a small convenience sample, for example, does not automatically support broad population-level conclusions.

## 3. Obtain ethics approval where required

Research involving people, personal data, confidential organisational material or sensitive topics may require review and approval by an institutional ethics committee. Follow the relevant institution’s rules before recruitment or data collection begins. Informed consent, voluntary participation, privacy, data security and the right to withdraw should be addressed in the approved process.

Do not assume that public availability of information removes all ethical or privacy obligations.

## 4. Keep evidence traceable

Maintain accurate records of data sources, collection dates, coding decisions, analysis steps and changes to the research plan. Distinguish observed findings from interpretation. Document limitations and avoid changing methods or excluding data simply to produce a preferred result.

Never fabricate or manipulate data, citations, peer-review activity or research records.

## 5. Treat authorship and citations responsibly

Authorship should reflect a meaningful scholarly contribution and follow the target institution’s or journal’s policy. Acknowledgement should not be used to imply authorship, and authorship should not be sold or granted as a favour.

Cite the original source for claims and quotations. Check that references exist, support the point being made and follow the journal’s style. AI tools, where used, should be handled according to the institution and journal’s current disclosure rules; responsibility for accuracy remains with the authors.

## 6. Report findings transparently

A credible manuscript explains its methods, presents results accurately, describes limitations and avoids claiming more than the evidence supports. Report relevant negative or inconclusive findings. Disclose funding, conflicts of interest and any required data or materials statements.

## 7. Check the journal before submission

Review the journal’s scope, editorial board, peer-review policy, indexing claims, fees, publication ethics and archiving arrangements using independent sources. Be cautious of unsolicited invitations that promise guaranteed or unusually rapid publication.

For journal and publisher guidance, consult the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) at [publicationethics.org](https://publicationethics.org/). The institution’s research office and target journal policies take precedence for any particular project.
