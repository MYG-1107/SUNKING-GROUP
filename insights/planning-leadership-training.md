## Begin with a workplace need

Before selecting a topic such as communication, delegation or change management, clarify the situation that needs attention. Are new managers unsure how to give feedback? Are project leads struggling to coordinate across departments? Is a team preparing for a change that has not been explained clearly?

Speak with the sponsor, managers and potential participants. Review relevant policies or process information where appropriate. Separate a skills gap from issues that require a change to roles, systems, resources or leadership expectations. Training cannot solve every organisational problem.

## Define observable outcomes

Avoid vague goals such as "become a better leader". Instead, describe what participants should be able to do. Examples include preparing a clear team briefing, delegating with agreed boundaries, conducting a structured feedback conversation or mapping stakeholders affected by a change.

Choose a small number of outcomes that match the available time. Make sure the organisation can provide opportunities to practise after the session.

## Design for practice

A balanced programme can combine a short concept introduction, an example, a case exercise, discussion, individual reflection and action planning. Role-play can be useful for sensitive conversations, provided participants understand the purpose and feel safe to practise.

Use scenarios that match the participants’ responsibilities. Avoid asking people to disclose confidential workplace situations in a public group. Offer a different activity when a topic is sensitive.

## Support transfer after the session

Before the programme ends, each participant should identify one action to try and the support needed to carry it out. Managers can reinforce learning by asking about the action in a one-to-one meeting, providing feedback and removing practical barriers.

Follow-up can take the form of a short group review, peer discussion or coaching session. Any follow-up should be planned and included in the original scope rather than assumed.

## Evaluate proportionately

A useful evaluation may include participant feedback, a practical skills demonstration and a follow-up discussion about whether the intended behaviour was attempted. If the organisation wants to assess wider business impact, it should agree an appropriate baseline, time period and data source. Avoid attributing a change in sales, retention or productivity to one workshop without examining other influences.

## A final planning checklist

Confirm the audience, purpose, learning outcomes, session length, facilitator, materials, accessibility needs, delivery format, fees, cancellation terms and post-session responsibilities. A clear plan gives participants a reason to engage and gives the organisation a realistic way to review what happened.
